You are a senior product designer. I need **three distinct visual concepts for a landing page**, delivered as **rendered PNG images** — full-page design comps, not code.

I've attached five screenshots of landing pages I admire. **They are references for ambition, craft level and component invention only — not for palette, typeface, or aesthetic.** Do not copy their colours or fonts. Look at how they construct things: how components are composed, how information is packed into cards, how headlines and interface elements interleave, how much visual confidence they carry. Then invent your own visual language for Envoy. I want three genuinely different answers, not one idea at three intensities.

## What Envoy actually is

Envoy is a planning tool for projects that need many outside vendors hired — a wedding, a home renovation, a company offsite, a fundraiser, a store opening.

The mechanic, precisely:

1. You describe your project in one plain-language sentence, plus a location. No forms, no category pickers.
2. Envoy works out **which categories of vendor the project actually needs** — and gives a reason for each one. For an outdoor autumn wedding it returns venue, catering, event rentals, florist, photography, music, officiant, bar service, hair & makeup, transportation — and explains, for instance, that rentals matter *because the site has no permanent cover and autumn evenings need heating*.
3. It finds real vendors in each category near you and ranks them.
4. It **writes the introduction emails for you**, in your voice, with your dates and details.
5. **Nothing sends until you approve it**, and messages send from *your* email address — so vendors reply directly to you, not into a marketplace inbox.
6. It tracks every thread in one place, so across an eight-week project you can always see who has replied, who is still out, and the small number of things that actually need your decision.

The competitive point: marketplaces (Thumbtack, Angi, The Knot, Houzz) work by *intermediating* — you post, they route, pros bid, the platform owns the conversation. Envoy inverts that. You own every relationship from the first email. Its existing tagline is *"Your digital representative for outreach."*

The product is **live**, with a free tier. It is also two-sided: there is a separate side for vendors who want to be found and to have Envoy help them respond to inquiries.

## Who the page is for

Someone running a **high-stakes, deadline-fixed, multi-category project, usually for the first and only time in their life**, without a professional coordinator. High stakes, moderate-to-low tooling sophistication. They are not evaluating software — they are trying to stop feeling behind.

The job they're hiring it for is not "find vendors" (Google finds vendors). It is: *"stop me from losing track, and make me feel like I'm running this instead of drowning in it."*

They arrive cold, usually from a friend's link or a founder's social post, with zero context and a little borrowed trust. So the page's first job is **disambiguation, not persuasion.**

Important: the audience is deliberately **broad**, not wedding-only. The page must let a bride, a homeowner mid-renovation, and an ops manager booking an offsite each recognise themselves within a second — without the page picking one and alienating the others.

## The one primary action

**Start a project — type it into the text entry field in the hero.** Free, no card.

Everything else on the page is secondary and must visibly recede: log in, pricing, the vendor side.

## Three problems the current site has — design against these

I audited the existing page. These are the three things that most need fixing, in priority order:

1. **It's abstract where it should be concrete, and written in procurement language.** It says things like "a project hub for renovations, events, commercial build-outs, and any multi-vendor project" and "Anyone running a multi-vendor project." Accurate; cold; and it describes a wedding in the register of a facilities contract. Abstraction — not breadth — is what makes it feel unwelcoming. The fix is concreteness and register, not narrowing the audience.
2. **It tells; it never shows, and gives no reason to believe.** The current page has zero images, zero product interface, zero proof. Every claim is a sentence. Meanwhile the product's most persuasive artifact — the reasoned vendor lineup — has never appeared on the site. Also note the page asks people to connect their personal email account, which is a large trust ask that the page currently does almost nothing to earn.
3. **Its structure prevents scanning.** It's seven full-screen scroll-snapped panels, so a visitor cannot skim, everything claims equal importance, and the same idea is stated three times in three consecutive sections.

## Required structure — exactly four sections

Not more. Each must do one job. A header and footer sit outside this count.

1. **Hero.** Must contain a **text entry field** where the visitor describes their project — this is non-negotiable and is the page's primary action. Show example prompts they could click to fill it. The hero should also make the product's output legible somehow, so the promise isn't purely verbal.
2. **Case studies.** Three projects, in this order: **an outdoor wedding for 150 guests · a 1920s kitchen renovation · a company offsite for 40 people.** This section carries the audience breadth and most of the page's warmth. It's also the natural place to show the product doing real work.
3. **Pricing.**
4. **For business** — the vendor side.

Fold the evidence into these four rather than adding sections for it. The vendor lineup, the project status view, and the draft-email-awaiting-approval are the three product artifacts worth showing; distribute them across the hero and case studies as you see fit.

## Real content — the words the page can use

These are the product's own words. Design around them rather than inventing claims.

**Lines worth keeping:**
- "Projects fail in the messy middle" — *supporting line:* "Not at the idea stage — when you're finding people, keeping details straight, and waiting for replies."
- "Not post-and-wait"
- "You're in control. Every message sends from your address only after you approve it."
- "Delegate outreach without losing your voice"
- "Email me this lineup to finish later" *(a low-commitment secondary CTA)*
- "Your digital representative for outreach."
- "Free to plan. Pay only to automate."

**The four pains, as the product states them:** searching across dozens of tabs · losing track of who you've contacted · writing the same intro email over and over · losing the thread as replies trickle in over weeks.

**Vendor side:** "Win more work, without chasing leads." · Three benefits: get discovered by ready buyers · never miss a lead again · let Envoy reply (connect your inbox, Envoy drafts responses, you approve before anything sends).

**Real pricing — use these exact figures:**
- **Free** — 1 active project · Envoy-built vendor lineups · reach out to vendors yourself · track every reply in one place
- **Pro, $96/yr** — everything in Free · Pro mode · unlimited projects
- **Agency, $200/yr per user** — everything in Pro · automated Envoy outreach · Envoy drafts replies and follow-ups in your voice · multiple seats · priority vendor responses

**Illustrative product data you may render** (label it visibly as an example — see rules below): an outdoor wedding for 150 guests near Austin returns Venue *(13 found)*, Catering *(8)*, Event rentals *(11)*, Florist *(9)*, Photography *(11)*, plus five more categories. A project at week five looks like: 23 contacts, 13 heard back, 5 still reaching out, 2 that need the owner's decision.

## Content integrity rules — these are absolute, and they apply inside the rendered image

This matters more for images than for text, because a rendered comp can easily fill itself with invented proof that looks real.

- **No invented social proof anywhere in the image.** No testimonials, no customer logo bars, no star ratings, no review counts, no user numbers, no press logos, no "trusted by" strips. None of these exist for this product. A logo wall in the comp is an instant fail.
- **No fabricated metrics** — no "10,000 projects planned," no "4.9 average rating," no growth charts with invented figures.
- If a layout wants proof, draw it as a **visibly empty, clearly labelled placeholder** — e.g. a bordered box reading `TESTIMONIAL — TBD`. Never realistic-looking fake content.
- **Any product interface drawn in the comp must carry a visible "example" label**, so nobody mistakes it for a live result.
- **Email only.** Do not show or imply SMS, text messages, or phone calls.
- **Don't overpromise automation in the hero.** Fully hands-off outreach is a paid tier. The hero can promise the lineup and the drafted emails; present full delegation as the upgrade.
- **No waitlist language** — the product is live.
- **Photography:** I have no image licence yet. Either design layouts that work without photos, or show photo areas as clearly-marked empty slots. Don't build a concept that collapses without imagery.

## What I want back

**Three PNG images — one per concept.** Each should be a **full-page desktop landing page comp**, top to bottom: header, all four sections, footer. Tall vertical composition, desktop proportions, as high-resolution as you can render.

If you can manage it without diluting the three main comps, a **companion mobile view** for each is welcome but optional.

Requirements for the comps themselves:

- **Legible text.** Headlines, section titles, button labels and pricing should be readable and correctly spelled. Body copy can be lighter-weight or partially abstracted if that renders more cleanly — I care most that the key lines and the structure read correctly.
- **Real structure, not a mood board.** I need to see actual layout decisions: hierarchy, spacing, component shapes, how the text entry field sits in the hero, how the case studies are arranged, how pricing is laid out.
- **Design the components, don't just place boxes.** The references show a high bar for component invention — cards that carry real information, interface fragments used as content, chips and pills doing real work. That's the level I'm after.

Alongside the images, give me a **one-paragraph thesis per concept**: what it's betting on, which of the three problems it solves best, and what it gives up. Be honest about the tradeoffs — "this one is riskier for a cold visitor" is more useful to me than a sales pitch.

Make the three genuinely distinct. **Distinct means structurally different, not the same layout in three colourways** — different ways of organising the argument, different component inventions, different ideas about what the hero is. If two converge, push one further apart before showing me.
